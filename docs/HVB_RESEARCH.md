# HUMANS VS BILLIONAIRES — Research Brief

*Grounded in Henry George's* Progress and Poverty *(1879). Every incident and quote is sourced; documented fact is distinguished from legend or dispute; where numbers vary, the scholarly range is given. The game dramatizes — this research does not invent.*

# CHAPTER 1: WILLIAM THE CONQUEROR (1066–1087)

## ORDERS
- **Harry the North into famine** — to: Norman knights and commanders. Order a scorched-earth winter campaign burning villages, food stores, and livestock across Yorkshire to break rebellion. Variables: repression +20, greed +10, hierarchy +15. Human cost: mass starvation; tens of thousands dead. Source: Orderic Vitalis, *Historia Ecclesiastica*; *History Today*, "The Harrying of the North."
- **Survey every hide of land** — to: royal commissioners. Commission the Domesday Book (1086), recording 13,000+ places, their holders, and taxable value so nothing escapes render. Variables: greed +15, regulation +10, hierarchy +10. Human cost: fixed feudal dues; peasants bound to assessed land. Source: Britannica, "Domesday Book"; Anglo-Saxon Chronicle.
- **Grant conquered land to Norman barons** — to: tenants-in-chief. Redistribute English estates: William granted lands directly to fewer than 180 men, making them his tenants-in-chief, keeping about one-fifth of England for himself, with the church holding a quarter and the tenants-in-chief roughly half. Variables: hierarchy +15, rent +15, speculation +5. Human cost: Anglo-Saxon lords dispossessed; native tenants pay new Norman lords. Source: Encyclopædia Britannica, "United Kingdom — The Normans, 1066–1154."
- **Impose forest law** — to: foresters and verderers. Designate royal hunting forests (e.g., the New Forest), criminalizing common use of woodland for firewood, grazing, and game. Variables: rent +10, repression +10, greed +10. Human cost: villages cleared; commoners punished for foraging. Source: standard Norman forest-law history (extent of clearance debated).
- **Build castles to hold the towns** — to: garrisons. Raise motte-and-bailey castles over conquered towns, often demolishing houses (two castles at York, 1068–69). Variables: repression +15, hierarchy +10. Human cost: urban dwellings razed for fortifications. Source: Norman Conquest scholarship.
- **Raise the geld (land tax)** — to: sheriffs. Increase the annual land tax announced around the Domesday survey. Variables: greed +10, rent +10. Human cost: heavier extraction from cultivators. Source: EBSCO, "Domesday Survey."
- **Endow abbeys as penance (PRESTIGE-LAUNDERING)** — to: clergy. Found and fund Battle Abbey on the Hastings battlefield and other religious houses to sanctify the conquest. Variables: prestige +15, hoard −5. Human cost: extraction relabeled as piety. Source: Norman ecclesiastical history.
- **Pardon adversaries on the deathbed (DE-ESCALATION)** — to: household. In 1087 release prisoners and pardon political enemies, including his half-brother Odo. Variables: repression −10, prestige +5, flareUps −5. Human cost: limited, late clemency after decades of severity. Source: David Bates, *William the Conqueror*; Mental Floss summary of Orderic.

## INCIDENTS
- **The Harrying of the North** — winter 1069–1070. Norman forces systematically burned crops, villages, and livestock across Yorkshire; famine followed. Orderic Vitalis put the dead "over 100,000" (roughly a quarter to a third of the North's population); Symeon of Durham wrote that no village remained inhabited between York and Durham for nine years. Modern historians accept catastrophe in the tens of thousands while doubting the round figure. Preceded by: rebellion + repression surge → repression≥80 & hierarchy≥60. Source: *History Today*; Orderic Vitalis.
- **Domesday "waste" in Yorkshire** — recorded 1086. Roughly two-thirds of Yorkshire settlements were still marked *vasta* ("waste") seventeen years after the Harrying. Preceded by: post-scorched-earth depopulation → crisis≥50 after a repression spike. Source: Domesday Book; *History Today*.
- **Clearing for the New Forest** — c.1079. Land in Hampshire was designated royal forest; later tradition (William of Malmesbury) held that villages were cleared for the king's hunting. Preceded by: rent-capture of common land → rent≥70 & greed≥60. Source: Norman forest-law history (clearance extent disputed).
- **Castles and devastation at York** — 1068–1069. Two castles were raised at York with houses cleared; the shire was devastated in the revolts and reprisals that triggered the Harrying. Preceded by: garrisoning against unrest → repression≥70. Source: Norman Conquest scholarship.

## RESISTANCE
- **Northern / Anglo-Danish rebellion** — 1069. Northumbrian and Anglo-Scandinavian rebels, joined by a Danish fleet, rallied around Edgar Ætheling and briefly retook York. Mechanism: violence / organizing. Source: Wikipedia, "Harrying of the North"; Kapelle.
- **Hereward the Wake / Isle of Ely** — 1070–1071. Anglo-Saxon rebels held out in the fens of Ely. Mechanism: violence (part-legend). Source: Norman chronicles.
- **Revolt of the Earls** — 1075. A conspiracy of Norman and Breton earls against William was suppressed. Mechanism: organizing (elite revolt). Source: Anglo-Saxon Chronicle.
- **The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle's dissent** — to c.1154. Monastic chroniclers recorded that "in his time men had much oppression and many injuries," preserving a written indictment of Norman rule. Mechanism: literacy / publication. Source: Anglo-Saxon Chronicle.

## CHARACTERS
- **William I ("the Conqueror" / "the Bastard")** (the billionaire-king) — ruthless warrior-administrator who turned conquest into a permanent rent system; his own chronicler has him descending on the North "like a raging lion." Source: Orderic Vitalis; David Bates.
- **Odo, Bishop of Bayeux** (half-brother / lieutenant) — regent and enforcer, later imprisoned then pardoned; commissioned the Bayeux Tapestry. Source: Norman Conquest histories.
- **Edgar Ætheling** (resister) — last male heir of the House of Wessex, figurehead of the northern revolt. Source: Wikipedia, "Harrying of the North."
- **Hereward the Wake** (resister) — Anglo-Saxon rebel leader in the Fens; part-legend, part-fact. Source: Norman chronicles.
- **Orderic Vitalis** (chronicler) — Anglo-Norman monk (b.1075) whose *Ecclesiastical History* condemned the Harrying and voiced William's remorse. Voice: pious, morally anguished. Source: *Historia Ecclesiastica*, c.1123–1142.

## ENDINGS
- **COLLAPSE:** William died on 9 September 1087 near Rouen after his horse threw him against the saddle pommel, rupturing his intestines. According to Orderic, his attendants stripped the room and fled, leaving the body "almost naked on the floor," and his bloated corpse reportedly burst at the funeral in Caen. The extraction system did not collapse — it hardened into English feudalism — but the conqueror's own end became a parable of vanity. Source: Orderic Vitalis; Mental Floss.
- **RECONSTITUTION:** The reform moment came generations later. The excesses of Norman forest law were partly rolled back by the Charter of the Forest (1217), a companion to Magna Carta that restored common rights — firewood, grazing (pannage), access — to royal forests. It is the nearest real analogue to "rent redirected to the commons." Source: Charter of the Forest, 1217 (constitutional history).

- "no village remained inhabited between York and Durham" — Symeon of Durham, *Libellus de exordio* (12th c.).
- "Truly, in his time men had much oppression and many injuries." — Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, entry on William I.

# CHAPTER 2: KING LEOPOLD II — CONGO FREE STATE (1885–1908)

## ORDERS
- **Set impossible rubber quotas** — to: concession-company agents (ABIR, Anversoise). Impose per-village wild-rubber quotas enforced on pain of death. Variables: greed +20, repression +15. Human cost: villagers abandoned farming, starved, were mutilated or killed for shortfalls. Source: E.D. Morel, *Red Rubber*; Adam Hochschild, *King Leopold's Ghost* (1998).
- **Account for every bullet with a hand** — to: Force Publique officers. Require soldiers to bring back a severed human hand for each cartridge fired, to prove ammunition wasn't wasted. Variables: repression +20, greed +10. Human cost: systematic mutilation; hands became a currency. Source: *Atrocities in the Congo Free State* (Casement Report; Hochschild).
- **Take hostages to force compliance** — to: Force Publique. Seize women and children until the men delivered rubber. Variables: repression +15, hierarchy +10. Human cost: captives held, starved, sometimes killed. Source: Casement Report (1904).
- **Grant monopoly concessions** — to: ABIR and other companies. Carve the territory into monopoly zones with their own armed militias. Variables: greed +15, regulation −15, rent +15. Human cost: privatized coercion across huge districts. Source: CEPR/VoxEU, "King Leopold's ghost."
- **Sign treaties with illiterate chiefs (SPECULATION)** — to: Henry Morton Stanley. Between 1879–1884 obtain 400+ "treaties" ceding sovereignty for cloth and trinkets. Variables: speculation +15, hierarchy +10. Human cost: land signed away without comprehension. Source: Teach Democracy; Grokipedia (Stanley expeditions).
- **Found the International African Association (PRESTIGE-LAUNDERING)** — to: diplomats and press. Cloak the venture as a scientific-humanitarian society to "suppress the slave trade" and win Berlin Conference recognition (1885) over ~2,350,000 km². Variables: prestige +20, regulation −10. Human cost: philanthropy as cover for extraction. Source: Wikipedia, "Congo Free State propaganda war."
- **Run a press syndicate to deny atrocities (PRESTIGE)** — to: paid journalists and lobbyists. Fund favorable press and bribe to discredit missionary reports. Variables: prestige +15, education −10, regulation −5. Human cost: truth suppressed as killing continued. Source: Twain, *King Leopold's Soliloquy* (1905); propaganda-war scholarship.
- **Appoint a Commission of Inquiry (HOLLOW DE-ESCALATION)** — to: state officials. In 1904–05 convene an inquiry to defuse the Casement Report — which confirmed abuses but left Leopold in control until 1908. Variables: regulation +10, repression −5, prestige +5. Human cost: cosmetic reform; extraction continued. Source: Casement Report aftermath; Wikipedia.

## INCIDENTS
- **Nsala and the hand and foot of Boali** — 1904, Nsongo district (ABIR concession). Sentries who found a village's rubber quota unmet killed, and reportedly cannibalized, Nsala's wife and five-year-old daughter, Boali; he was photographed staring at her severed hand and foot. Preceded by: quota unmet + enforcement → greed≥80 & repression≥70. Source: E.D. Morel, *King Leopold's Rule in Africa* (1904); Alice Seeley Harris photograph.
- **Baskets of severed hands** — c.1900–1904. Missionaries and Casement documented Force Publique soldiers and villagers delivering baskets of smoked hands as proof of kills. Preceded by: bullet-accounting policy → repression≥80. Source: Casement Report (1904); Hochschild.
- **The Abir district killings** — 1903. A rubber expedition led to the shooting of at least 122 Congolese; one Belgian official later received a five-year sentence. Preceded by: quota enforcement → greed≥80 & repression≥70. Source: Casement Report; Cambridge, "Roger Casement and the Congo."
- **Sleeping-sickness collapse** — reported for 1901. The Wikipedia article "Atrocities in the Congo Free State" states an estimated 500,000 Congolese died of sleeping sickness that year as displacement and forced labor spread disease among weakened populations. **[Flag: this single-year Congo figure is weakly sourced; the best-documented contemporaneous epidemic is Uganda's, where over a quarter-million people died 1901–1905 (Cambridge History of Africa). Treat the 500,000 figure as disputed.]** Preceded by: agricultural collapse under quotas → crisis≥60 & wages at survival. Source: Wikipedia summary; Cambridge History of Africa.
- **Population halved** — 1880s–1920. Hochschild and anthropologist Jan Vansina estimate the population fell by roughly 10 million, about half; Vansina and others caution against extrapolating from the worst rubber districts to the whole territory. Preceded by: sustained extraction → crisis≥70. Source: Hochschild (1998); Vansina (2010); CEPR. **[Estimates disputed; scholarly range spans lower figures to ~10 million.]**

## RESISTANCE
- **The Casement Report** — Feb 1904. British consul Roger Casement's eyewitness report to Parliament documented killings, mutilations, and hostage-taking, with witness statements appended (forty pages plus twenty of testimony). Mechanism: publication / literacy. Source: Casement Report, Cd. 1933 (1904).
- **Congo Reform Association** — founded 23 March 1904 in Liverpool by E.D. Morel with Casement. Used shipping-record analysis, lantern-slide lectures, and photographs; became arguably the first modern international human-rights movement, with supporters including Mark Twain, Arthur Conan Doyle, Joseph Conrad, and Booker T. Washington. Mechanism: publication / organizing. Source: Wikipedia, "Congo Reform Association."
- **Morel's shipping-record exposé** — from c.1900. As a Liverpool shipping clerk, Morel noticed ships returned from Congo full of rubber and ivory but sailed out carrying mainly guns and chains — proof of forced labor, not trade. Mechanism: literacy / data analysis. Source: Grokipedia summary; Hochschild.
- **Force Publique / Batetela mutinies** — 1895–1897. African soldiers of the Force Publique mutinied (notably at Luluabourg), fighting the regime for years. Mechanism: violence. Source: Congo Free State military history.
- **Missionary testimony (Sheppard, Harris, Guinness)** — 1890s–1900s. American and British missionaries gathered testimony and photographs; William Sheppard documented ravaged villages in the Kasai. Mechanism: publication. Source: Congo Reform scholarship.

## CHARACTERS
- **Leopold II** (the billionaire-king) — never set foot in Congo yet owned it personally; ruled through humanitarian rhetoric while extracting rubber fortunes. Voice: unctuous, self-justifying piety. Source: Hochschild.
- **Henry Morton Stanley** (agent / lieutenant) — explorer who secured the founding treaties; known to the Congolese as "Bula Matari" (breaker of rocks). Source: Teach Democracy.
- **Nsala of Wala** (victim) — grieving father whose child Boali was mutilated and killed; his photographed grief became the movement's icon. Source: Morel (1904).
- **Roger Casement** (chronicler / resister) — British consul whose 1904 report exposed the system; later executed by Britain in 1916 for his role in the Irish Easter Rising. Voice: precise, documentary. Source: Casement Report.
- **E.D. Morel** (resister / chronicler) — Liverpool shipping clerk turned crusading journalist; founder of the CRA. Voice: data-driven moral outrage. Source: Wikipedia; Hochschild.
- **William Sheppard** (chronicler) — African-American Presbyterian missionary who documented atrocities in the Kasai. Source: Congo Reform scholarship.

## ENDINGS
- **COLLAPSE:** Under sustained pressure from the Casement Report and Morel's Congo Reform Association — amplified by Twain, Conan Doyle, and Booker T. Washington — Belgium annexed the Congo Free State from Leopold in 1908, ending his personal rule; he died in 1909 to public booing at his funeral. Extraction continued under Belgian colonial rule, but the mask-off private-atrocity system ended. Source: Wikipedia; Hochschild.
- **RECONSTITUTION:** The reform moment was the international campaign itself — the Casement Report (1904) and the Congo Reform Association (1904–1913), which dissolved itself in 1913 believing its work done. It stands as history's first successful mass human-rights campaign driven by publication and testimony (education corrodes prestige). Source: Congo Reform Association (Wikipedia); Cambridge.

# CHAPTER 3: GILDED AGE AMERICA (1870s–1890s)

## ORDERS
- **Cut wages, break the union** — to: Henry Clay Frick (Homestead). Impose an 18% wage cut and refuse to deal with the Amalgamated Association. Variables: greed +20, repression +15. Human cost: skilled steelworkers locked out; families' livelihoods cut. Source: Britannica, "Homestead Strike"; AFL-CIO.
- **Hire Pinkertons to retake the plant** — to: Pinkerton National Detective Agency. Bring ~300 armed agents up the Monongahela by barge to protect strikebreakers. Variables: repression +20. Human cost: pitched battle; workers and agents killed. Source: History.com, "Homestead Strike."
- **Slash railroad wages 10%** — to: B&O and other rail managers (1877). Cut wages during depression while paying dividends. Variables: greed +15, wages −15. Human cost: brakemen at ~$1.75/day for 12-hour shifts; strikes and deaths followed. Source: Howard Zinn via libcom; Britannica.
- **Corner the gold market (SPECULATION)** — to: Jay Gould and Jim Fisk (1869). Buy gold and bribe officials to stop Treasury sales, spiking the price. Variables: speculation +20, greed +10, crisis +10. Human cost: the Black Friday panic ruined many investors. Source: Wikipedia, "Jay Gould"; American Aristocracy.
- **Withhold railroad land grants (SPECULATION)** — to: railroad land offices. Hold federally granted land off the market to sell later at appreciated prices. Variables: speculation +20, rent +15. Human cost: settlers priced out or forced into tenancy. Source: Gilded Age railroad land-grant history.
- **Deploy state militia against strikers** — to: governors. Call in militia and federal troops to reopen the rails in 1877. Variables: repression +20, hierarchy +10. Human cost: ~100 dead nationwide; 61 killed in Pittsburgh. Source: Wikipedia, "Pittsburgh railroad strike of 1877."
- **Give away libraries (PRESTIGE-LAUNDERING)** — to: Andrew Carnegie's foundations. Between 1883 and 1929 Carnegie funded 2,509 libraries worldwide (1,689 in the US), giving some $56 million; by 1919 his grants had built nearly half of the roughly 3,500 libraries in the United States. Variables: prestige +20, hoard −5. Human cost: philanthropy after Homestead's blood. Source: Carnegie Corporation of New York.
- **Refuse to negotiate (REFUSED DE-ESCALATION)** — to: Frick. After the battle, declare "under no circumstances will we have any further dealing with the Amalgamated Association." Variables: repression +10, hierarchy +10, flareUps +10. Human cost: union destroyed; open shop imposed. Source: AFL-CIO.

## INCIDENTS
- **The Battle of Homestead** — 6 July 1892. Some 300 Pinkertons on barges exchanged gunfire with thousands of strikers on the banks of the Monongahela; the day left seven workers and three Pinkertons dead (LOC records ~16 total). Frick was later shot and stabbed by anarchist Alexander Berkman but survived. Preceded by: wage cut + lockout + private army → greed≥80 & repression≥70. Source: History.com; AFL-CIO; LOC.
- **The Great Railroad Strike / Pittsburgh** — July 1877. After a 10% wage cut, strikes spread nationwide; in Pittsburgh, militia fire and rioting killed an estimated 61 people (including women and children), and strikers burned the Union Depot and 39 buildings, 120+ locomotives, and 1,200+ rail cars. Preceded by: wage cut in depression → wages≤survival & repression≥70. Source: Wikipedia; Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia.
- **Black Friday gold panic** — 24 September 1869. Gould and Fisk's attempted gold corner collapsed when the Treasury released gold, ruining speculators and rippling through the economy. Preceded by: speculation mania → speculation≥80 & crisis≥50. Source: Wikipedia, "Jay Gould."
- **Panic of 1873** — began 18 September 1873 with the failure of Jay Cooke & Company over Northern Pacific railroad speculation, driving a quarter of railroads into bankruptcy and a "Long Depression." Preceded by: railroad speculation bubble → speculation≥80 & crisis≥70. Source: Smithsonian Magazine.
- **"A rifle diet for a few days"** — 1877. Pennsylvania Railroad's Thomas A. Scott suggested giving hungry strikers "a rifle diet… and see how they like that kind of bread." Preceded by: labor unrest + repression → repression≥70. Source: Pressbooks, "Capital and Labor."

## RESISTANCE
- **Homestead strike** — July–Nov 1892. The Amalgamated Association held the town for months before defeat; it inspired the ballad "Father Was Killed by the Pinkerton Men." Mechanism: organizing / violence. Source: AFL-CIO.
- **Henry George's 1886 mayoral run** — 1886. Running for the United Labor Party on the single-tax platform, George won 68,110 votes (31.01%), beating Theodore Roosevelt (60,435) and finishing second to Abram Hewitt (90,552) amid fraud allegations. Mechanism: organizing / publication. Source: Wikipedia, "1886 New York City mayoral election."
- **Single Tax clubs & The Standard** — 1887–1892. George built a national network of Single Tax clubs and published the *New York Standard* to spread *Progress and Poverty*'s ideas. Mechanism: literacy / publication. Source: NYPL, Henry George papers.
- **Ida Tarbell's Standard Oil exposé** — 1902–1904. Her 19-part *McClure's* series exposed Rockefeller's monopoly practices and fed the 1911 breakup. Mechanism: publication. Source: Britannica, "Ida Tarbell." (Published just past the core window but the same reform current.)
- **Great Railroad Strike** — 1877. The first nationwide labor upheaval; ~100,000 workers struck spontaneously. Mechanism: organizing / violence. Source: Britannica.

## CHARACTERS
- **Andrew Carnegie** (the billionaire) — steel magnate who preached the "Gospel of Wealth" and funded libraries while his manager broke the union; absent in Scotland during Homestead. Voice: benevolent self-justification. Source: Britannica.
- **Henry Clay Frick** (lieutenant / enforcer) — Carnegie's operations chief who ordered the lockout, the fence, and the Pinkertons. Voice: implacable. Source: History.com.
- **Jay Gould** (the billionaire-speculator) — railroad and gold manipulator, "most duplicitous of the robber barons." Voice: cold, calculating. Source: Wikipedia.
- **Alexander Berkman** (resister) — anarchist who shot and stabbed Frick in 1892; imprisoned 14 years. Voice: revolutionary. Source: libcom.
- **Henry George** (resister / chronicler) — economist whose *Progress and Poverty* (1879) and 1886 campaign made land rent a mass political issue. Voice: moral, plainspoken. Source: Britannica Money.
- **Ida Tarbell** (chronicler) — muckraking journalist who dismantled Standard Oil's myth. Voice: meticulous, evidence-driven. Source: Britannica.

## ENDINGS
- **COLLAPSE:** The era's boom-bust logic culminated in the Panic of 1873 (a quarter of railroads bankrupt; a "Long Depression") and the Panic of 1893, when — by high estimates — roughly 17–19% of the workforce was unemployed at the peak, with some estimates near 20% in the first year. These were speculation-triggered crises exactly as Henry George described: land and rail values inflated on anticipation, then crashed, dragging labor down with them. Source: Smithsonian; Encyclopedia.com; Wikipedia.

George's remedy (tax land rent for the public) was never enacted, but his diagnosis shaped the reform. Source: Britannica; NYPL.

# THE TEN LITMUS TESTS

*Ten questions a citizen can ask to tell whether an economy serves humans or billionaires, each grounded in George's analysis.*

1. **Who collects the rent?** Does the value created by the whole community — rising land, network, or platform value — flow to a public fund, or into a private hoard?
2. **Can ordinary labor still reach the margin?** Is there free or affordable land, housing, or opportunity a working person can reach without first paying tribute to an owner?
3. **What share of your life goes to permission-to-exist?** How much of your wages is captured as rent (housing, land, access fees) before you keep anything for the work you did?
4. **Is wealth taxed where it's earned or where it's hidden?** Does the system tax unearned land and monopoly value, or does it tax labor while letting rent and speculation run free?
5. **Is land — or its modern equivalent — withheld for speculation?** Are homes, land, or essential platforms kept idle or scarce on purpose, priced for tomorrow instead of used today?
6. **Who pays for the enforcers?** Are police, private guards, and "content moderation" funded to protect people — or to protect extraction from the people?
7. **Can the truth be published and read?** Is the press free and literacy strong enough that exposés actually corrode the powerful's prestige — or is dissent suppressed and truth bought off?
8. **Does philanthropy substitute for justice?** Are museums, libraries, soup kitchens, and "missions to save humanity" laundering a fortune, or is the underlying extraction actually being stopped?
9. **When crisis hits, who is protected?** In the speculation-triggered crash, are the hoards insulated while wages and savings are wiped out — or is the loss shared?
10. **Does the law bend to bribery?** Is regulation real friction on extraction, or is it corroded by money until the rules apply only to those who can't afford to break them?
